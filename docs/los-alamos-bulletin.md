## What this source contributes

The introduction explains why Los Alamos occupies such a prominent place in popular accounts of the Manhattan Project, while stressing that it was only one site in a much larger enterprise. It presents badges as access-control artifacts as well as portraits and challenges a history concentrated on famous white male physicists. It also frames interpretations of Oppenheimer's or Feynman's expressions as questions—not verified psychological findings.

The app now links to the feature from a research spotlight after “Behind the badges.” The short description is an original paraphrase. Following the user’s request to use the montage, a reduced reference image is now included with attribution; the article text is not reproduced. See the follow-up below.

## Scope and extraction limits

The accessible page extraction returned the introductory montage and introduction, with a “1 / 1” slide indicator. Although the text describes a curated selection of individual stories, those individual slide captions were not exposed by the extraction used in this review.

Therefore:

- This is **not** a complete employee list, a badge-issue ledger, or an authenticated numeric ordering.
- No unseen slide captions, person names, occupations, or badge identifiers have been reconstructed.
- The feature supplies context, not proof of an individual badge holder's clearance, motives, ethnicity, duties, or travel to another Manhattan Project site.
- Existing badge records retain their original file-level sources. The curated record count is unchanged.
- The Bulletin montage’s compilation-specific reuse rights have not been established. A reduced reference is now included at the user’s explicit request, with attribution and a reuse notice; it is not labeled public domain.

## Editorial changes and research priorities

The feature reinforces an evidence-first approach to expanding beyond famous scientists. The app now states that a photograph cannot establish temperament, motives, ethnicity, or clearance.

The following **existing research leads** are supported by the Nuclear Museum's computing history, not attributed to unseen Bulletin slides:

1. **Mary Frankel:** investigate her documented work as a human computer, locate her badge, and verify its visible identifier.
2. **Jean Bacher and Kay Manley:** use their oral histories and identified badge photographs to document computing work and daily life.
3. **Eldred Nelson and Donald Flanders:** continue the computing-organization thread while preserving the distinction between organizing work and performing it.

Lead source: https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/history/human-computers-los-alamos/

None of these leads is promoted to a new curated badge record by this update. Each needs its own image, provenance review, and transcription.
