## 0. The "Diane" cipher is the Diana cryptosystem

No hand cipher named *Diane* exists. Searches for "Diane cipher", "Diane code" and
"Diane hand cipher" return only a person named Diane in a Cipher Mysteries comment
thread; the repository has no `diane` reference either. The requested cipher is the
**Diana cryptosystem**: an NSA-developed one-time-pad trigraph used by US Army Special
Forces in Vietnam (and by allied services afterwards), the last physical wheel/table
cipher issued for field Morse traffic. Sources reviewed 2026-10-14:

* blogbyben.com, "Trigraphs, Diana Pads and Zombies" (2014-12-08) — SF Morse traffic,
  pads of random five-letter groups (30 words per page), and the property that any three
  letters of a trio close in **every** order (`BKO`, `KOB`, `OBK`, `BOK`, …).
* krypto431 / CADCRYS (sa6mwa) — reciprocal table (normal alphabet against the reversed
  alphabet), same column for encrypt and decrypt, `Z` as operator/fill.
* courtneyimbert/diana — a Python OTP implementation described as "similar to Vernam or
  Vigenère"; zappi-tkn/Diana — Diana followed by a columnar transposition.
* creativecrafthouse.com — the US Army Special Forces cipher disk ("the last physical
  wheel-based cipher used by the U.S. military").
* **FM 31-20, *Special Forces Operational Techniques* (Headquarters, Department of the Army,
  30 December 1965), chapter 9 "Communications", paragraphs 108–118, read from the scanned
  text at archive.org (item `FM_31_20_S_F_O_T_1965`, public domain, 540 pages).** This is the
  manual that governed the detachments that used the pads, and it fixes the working
  environment the forum testimony describes. §111a: "Radio personnel assigned to Special
  Forces operational detachments are confronted with problems different from those faced by
  radio operators assigned to a conventional military unit. … **Messages are encrypted using
  pencil and paper rather than machine systems.**" §114 breaks communication security into
  physical, transmission and cryptographic security, and adds the handling rules that a
  26-page-pad system implies: cryptographic material is carried on the person or under
  constant guard (§114b(2)), "Information on cryptographic systems used by Special Forces is
  never released to indigenous personnel" (§114b(1)), "Detachment cryptographic systems and
  SOI's must not fall into enemy hands … destruction by burning is not complete unless the
  ashes are destroyed" (§114b(3)), and "Specific instructions, techniques, and methods to be
  used are covered in premission briefings on a need-to-know basis" (§114d) — which is
  precisely why the pad's own form is documented by testimony rather than by a manual scan.
  (The same chapter's §111b sets the Morse standard at 18 wpm under ACP-124B, and §109c
  lists the clandestine means.)
* sfrvn.net (Special Forces in Vietnam, C-1 commo history) — the operational procedure and
  the pad layout described above; crypto.stackexchange (2019-06-16) resolves that the
  "5-letter words" are independently drawn letters, not dictionary words.
* Programming Praxis, "Diana Cryptosystem" (2014-12-19) — the worked example reproduced
  below, including the two clear indicator groups.

Operational procedure (Special Forces in Vietnam, C-1 comms section — sfrvn.net, and
the same account quoted by Programming Praxis and the crypto.stackexchange discussion of
those pads):

* The pads were a matched *tablet pair*; each sheet was pre-printed with **five five-letter
  random groups per line** (30 groups to a page, so six lines), generated by picking each
  letter independently — "words" in the field slang, but not dictionary words.
* The sending operator keyed the message **over** the pad groups and published the **first
  two groups of the line used, unencrypted**, as the indicator; the receiving station found
  the matching line from them and worked the trigraph back to plaintext.
* With one page per A-Team and the headquarters copy, the same sheet was never reused, which
  is what made the system a one-time pad rather than a repeating-key cipher.

Mechanics implemented (`diana`, `dianaTable`):

```
c = 25 − (k + p) mod 26       k = pad/key letter, p = plaintext letter
```

The full field procedure is a `clear` argument on the `diana` recipe (two clear groups plus
the published example's `XYZ` filler reproduce the textbook output exactly):

```
pad line  UKVTF WZHOK GORWY WETFR COYET
plain     ATTAC KATDA WNXYZ            (ATTACK AT DAWN + filler)
sent      UKVTF WZHOK TSPDZ TVNRI BYEXH
```

Control for that vector: Programming Praxis, "Diana Cryptosystem" (2014-12-19), which prints
the same three lines; the arithmetic is the same reciprocal table as `table[B][K] = O`.

Because `25 − (k + p)` is symmetric in `k` and `p`, the table is **reciprocal**: the same
entry enciphers and deciphers, and a trio `{a,b,c}` closes in all six orders. Control
vector used in the tests: `table[B][K] = O`, and all six orders of `{B, K, O}` must close.
`dianaTable` prints the full 26×26 worksheet and self-tests all 676 entries for
reciprocity (`PASS`).

One question the published example invites: the first two pad groups travel in clear, so can
the rest of the line be reconstructed from them? No — check it arithmetically. Treating the
ten clear letters as a repeating key and working `25 − (k + p)` over the 15 known plaintext
letters agrees with the transmitted groups at **1 of 15** positions, and taking the three
unknown pad groups to be any simple function of the clear letters and the ciphertext (`25−(a+b)`,
`a+b`, `a−b`, `b−a`) recovers **0, 2, 1 and 2 of 15** letters respectively, against a random-key
baseline of ≈1/26 (measured 2,866/75,000 = 0.038 over 5,000 random 15-letter pairs). The example's
five groups are independent draws, which is exactly what makes the clear indicator harmless as
long as the pad is one-time: the two clear groups identify *which* line was used, not what the
other three letters are. The real risk in the field was pad capture, not the indicator.

Design notes:

* Pad/key letters repeat for demonstration only; in the field the pad is one-time and
  destroyed after use. The recipe description says so, and the operation exists to teach
  the arithmetic and the trio property, not to protect traffic.
* Case and non-letters pass through unchanged so formatted messages stay readable;
  `group=5` collapses the output to classic five-letter groups.
* `diana` is not the same as the kitchen's existing `vic`/`straddlingCheckerboard`
  digit encodings — Diana never leaves the 26 letters.

## 1. OD poem code — from key worksheet to full message

The kitchen already had `odPoemKey` (indicator/key worksheet: poem, word positions,
secret → words, key, ranks, indicator, check group). What was missing was the message
stage. `odPoemExact` now performs the complete procedure:

1. Number the first 20 words of the agreed poem.
2. Key words are picked by position; the key is their concatenation
   (`ONZE WAS ZIEN ALLE` → `ONZEWASZIENALLE` by the published example).
3. Message is uppercased, `.` becomes `STOP`, spaces are dropped, the random three-letter
   callsign is appended, and pad letters are prepended until the length is a multiple of
   five (the published example pads `QW` to reach 130 letters = 26 groups).
4. A columnar transposition keyed by those words produces the groups.
5. The indicator hides the word positions under the pre-agreed five-digit secret
   (`A=1…Z=26`, zero-padded left to five slots), and the check group is the indicator
   put through the published two-column swap (C↔M, R↔H, U–Z unchanged).

Control vector (Crypto Museum, Ton van Schendel / Ordedienst worksheet):

| Element | Published | Kitchen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Words | ONZE(2) WAS(6) ZIEN(11) ALLE(15) | same |
| Secret | `58265` | same |
| Indicator | `EJHQT` | `EJHQT` |
| Message | 26 groups, starting `DTAOR AAROC FATSA FUEWH` and ending `… SFPAD EITNU` | exact, all 26 groups |
| Check | `OTRGJ` | `OTRGJ` |

Two source quirks are recorded rather than reproduced:

* The Crypto Museum worked row computes the fourth position as **14** in the arithmetic
  display while its prose (and the resulting indicator `EJHQT`) require **15**. The
  kitchen uses ALLE = 15, which is what reproduces the published indicator.
* The published message only reaches 130 letters with the two pad letters included; the
  plaintext itself is 128. The recipe states the pad it inserted so the arithmetic is
  auditable, and on decryption it strips the pad (toggleable) and reports the check
  comparison — a wrong check group is printed as `MISMATCH — expected …, got …`.

Not to be confused with the kitchen's `soePoemCode` (SOE/SAS poem code, Jabberwocky
default, single/double transposition), which is a different procedure from the same era.

## 2. VIC cipher — schedule and message stage, both against the published example

The kitchen's `vic` operation was checkerboard-only (text ↔ digits). The deep dive added
the other two halves of the cipher:

* `vicSchedule` — the key schedule, labelled line by line the way the CIA analysis does:
  `A` keygroup, `B` date, `C = A − B` **without borrowing**, `D` first 20 phrase letters,
  `E.1/E.2` sequencing of each ten-letter half, `F.1` five chain digits appended to `C`,
  `F.2` the aide row, `G = E.1 + F.1` digit-wise mod 10, `H` = `G` encoded under `E.2`
  (the ten digits that carry the real strength), `J` the sequencing of `H` (column order),
  `K–P` the five ten-digit lines from 50 chain digits, then `Q` (columnar key) and
  `R` (diagonal key) as the first `a` and next `b` digits of the column read, where `a`
  and `b` come from the last two non-equal digits of `P` plus the personal number, and
  finally `S`, the checkerboard header row.
* `vicExact` — message stage: bisect the plaintext and swap the halves (optional literal
  marker), straddling-checkerboard encode with the `S` header, pad to fives, optional
  columnar (`Q`) and diagonal/triangular-disrupted (`R`) transpositions, optional
  cleartext keygroup insertion `P` groups from the end. `decrypt` inverts each step in
  reverse order and strips up to four trailing pad digits.

Control vector (published worked example: personal number 6, date `139195`, phrase
"Twas the night before Christmas", keygroup `72401`):

| --- | --- | --- |
| C | `69592` | `69592` |
| E.1 / E.2 | `8017942653` / `6013589427` | same |
| F.1 / F.2 | `6959254417` / `1234567890` | same |
| G | `4966196060` | `4966196060` |
| H | `3288628787` | `3288628787` |
| J | `3178429506` | `3178429506` |
| K / L / M / N / P | `5064805552` / `5602850077` / `1620350748` / `7823857125` / `5051328370` | same |
| Key lengths | 13 and 6 | 13 and 6 |
| Q | `0668005552551` | `0668005552551` |
| R | `758838` | `758838` |
| S (checkerboard header) | `5961328470` | `5961328470` |
| Message code | 99 digits, `602538000055500000080873198000099911155580677642881866676667549976028759956964596658338765886658337` | exact |

Checkerboard encoding rules that had to be rediscovered from the vector (each one is a
silent-failure trap, so they are asserted in the test):

* The eight single-digit letters are the second row `AT ONE SIR` — the two blanks become
  the row heads (here `6` and `8`); the remaining 28 symbols fill the two ten-column rows.
* Digits are sent as **three identical digits**, wrapped by the `/` (class `80`) shift
  symbol before and after each number run.
* Spaces are dropped entirely: VIC has no space symbol, so word division is read by
  sense. The published message uses `/` as a visual half-marker in the article; it is not
  a cipher symbol, and inserting it desynchronises everything after it.
* The bisected halves are concatenated with no separator. With spaces and the article's
  visual `/` removed, the published message is 55 characters and is cut **20** into it:
  `ATTACKATDAWN.BYDAWNI` + `MEAN0500.NOT0915LIKEYOUDIDLASTTIME.`, and the halves are sent
  swapped. Getting this cut wrong is invisible until the last digit, which is exactly why
  it is an asserted test vector.

Three implementation bugs were found and fixed by exactly this vector, and are worth
remembering: (1) chain addition must *extend* the seed rather than re-deriving from the
original digits (symptom: lines K–P all shifted, widths 11/8); (2) sequencing must treat
`0` as **10** (sorts last) with ties resolved left-to-right (symptom: `S` came out
`7183540692` instead of `5961328470`); (3) lines `Q`/`R` are read column by column under
`J` in digit order 1…10 **continuing across** columns (symptom: `R` wrong while `Q` was
right).

Round-trip evidence in the test: the message `ATTACK AT DAWN …` cut at 20, double
transposition and keygroup insertion produces 20 complete five-digit groups containing
the keygroup `72401`, and decrypting that ciphertext with the same key material recovers
the plaintext exactly, with the two pad digits identified and removed.

A second, fully worked VIC example exists (Basic Cryptography, "The Soviet Spy Hand Cipher

short phrase `a sin to er`, 20-letter phrase "put on your thinking cap", date `921945`
(2 Sept 1945), agent number 05, message ID `54213`, message "You have been discovered destroy
the document and retreat" — so it is an independent check of the same algorithm:

| Stage | Published | Kitchen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sequencing of both phrase halves (A, B) | `4962103857`, `4587693210` | same |
| Line-E expansion F, G, H | `6812949313`, `0774042160`, `0337075490` | same |
| Line-J (sequencing of H, 0 valued at 10) | `8125964370` | same |
| 50-digit chain block (I) and its column read | `3607729393…`, `66348 07147 32489 …` | same |
| Widths from the last two non-equal digits (6, 7) + agent 05 | 11 and 12 | 11 and 12 |
| First transposition (plain columnar, key = first 11 block digits) | `431168 5592445 237414 …` | reproduced exactly |
| Second (diagonal) transposition | see below | corrected |

The first transposition reproduces digit for digit, and so does everything up to it, which
pins the plain-transposition stage to a second independent vector.

The *second* transposition is the reason this example matters: it exposed a real defect. The
deep dive originally reused the SECOM triangular construct for VIC's diagonal transposition,
which round-trips with itself but is not the VIC rule. Four independent implementations agree
on the rule actually used by VIC:

* `kyrias/vic` (Python) — `build_disruption_table`: the left (undisrupted) part of each row
  starts at the column labelled with the smallest key value and grows one column per row,
  restarting at the next label in ascending order; the grid is filled left parts → short row
  → right parts and read out column by column in label order.
* `pavelanni/vic-cipher` (Go) — `ALGORITHM.md`: "the disruption starts at the column whose key
  value is smallest … a right-triangle of right cells that expands one column per row".
* `Csi18nAlistairMann/vic-cipher` (PHP, from the CIA "Two Soviet Spy Cyphers" figures) —
  `generateDisruptionData()` walks columns 1, 2, … and grows the undisrupted width by one per
  row.
* `crunchbangle/vic-ts` (TypeScript) — `getDiagonalLengths()`, whose published test vector is
  reproduced here: key `536124`, 60 digits → left widths `3,4,5,6,4,5,6,1,2,3`, and the
  diagonalised grid reads out as
  `pGKPVbg269qsLQvch37YBEIOTXe0lnrtuRwxi48ZADHMSWdjkmCFJNUaf15o`.

The kitchen now uses that rule (`fdVicLeftWidths`, `fdVicDiagEnc`, `fdVicDiagDec`), and the
blog's own illustrated pattern — width 6 with labels 1, 2, 3 in columns 2, 4, 1, giving rows
`2,3,4,5,6 | 4,5,6 | 1,2,3,4,5,6` — is pinned by a test as well.

**The primary source for the rule.** David Kahn's CIA article "Number One From Moscow" (1993,
reissued from his 1960 booklet *Two Soviet Spy Ciphers*) describes the second tableau's
disruption directly:

> "The first D area begins in the top row under keynumber 1 and runs to the right side of that
> row. In each of the following rows, it begins one column to the right. When the columns are
> exhausted, one row is skipped and another D area is started in the following row with the
> column under keynumber 2, and so forth for as many rows as are needed to accommodate all the
> cipher digits." … "The cipher digits taken vertically from the first tableau are inscribed
> horizontally from left to right into the rows of the second tableau, but leaving the D areas
> blank. When the non-D portions of all rows have been filled, the remaining digits are written
> in from left to right in the D areas, starting with the top row."

That is exactly what the kitchen's triangle construct implements, and a test now proves the
two partitions are identical cell-for-cell for the 2016 example's key (55 disrupted cells).
Equivalently, the *left* (non-D) widths implied by that partition are the vector published by
`crunchbangle/vic-ts` — `3,4,5,6,4,5,6,1,2,3` for key `536124` and 60 digits — which is why
the four implementations agree: they describe the same staircase from the two sides.

The rule is not only textual: the same triangle construct drives the SECOM side of the
kitchen, whose exact operation reproduces the **official SECOM 105-digit ciphertext** (plaintext
"RV TOMORROW AT 1400PM TO COMPLETE TRANSACTION USE DEADDROP AS USUAL", phrase "MAKE NEW FRIENDS
BUT KEEP THE OLD") — pinned by `tests/secom-exact.test.mjs` and re-run by
`tools/secom_probe.py`. Disabling the disruption makes that published vector fail, so a
published field ciphertext independently validates the partition the VIC code uses.

Kahn's article also supplies the key material of the hollow-nickel message (date 391945, so
Line B `39194`; keygroup `20818`; personal number 13; phrase the first 20 letters of the song
"The Lone Accordion"), and three text-only micro-vectors that are now pinned by tests:

**The one table that does not follow the primary-source rule** is the 2016 blog's own second

## 3. Gronsfeld and SECOM

**Gronsfeld** (`gronsfeld`, existing) is the numeric-key Vigenère (`C = P + k mod 26`).
The deep dive added `gronsfeldAnalyze` because the weakness is the point: a digit key has
only ten candidates per column, so the analyser ranks key lengths 1…12 by average column
index of coincidence and then solves each column by English letter frequency. Control:
the analyser recovers key `3141` and the exact plaintext from 61 letters of ciphertext
produced by the kitchen's own `gronsfeld`, with the true length (`4`, IoC ≈ 0.067, the
English value) ranked first.

**SECOM** already had its full implementation (`secomSchedule`, `secomExact`) added with
the previous field-cipher work and verified against the 105-digit publisher example in
`tools/secom_probe.py`. While this dive was in progress, `main` independently landed the same
"zero is ten" rule as a fix on the SECOM side (`sxDigitSeq` sequences a numeric row in the
order 1…9,0 instead of letting JavaScript's lexical sort put `0` first), guarded by
`tests/secom-exact.test.mjs`; this branch merged that fix, so the VIC `S` line and the SECOM
board row are now the two places where the rule is pinned by tests. While this dive was in progress, `main` independently landed the
same class of fix on the SECOM side — `sxDigitSeq` sequences a numeric row in the order
`1…9,0` instead of letting JavaScript's lexical sort put `0` first — with
`tests/secom-exact.test.mjs` as its guard; this branch merged that fix, and the VIC `S`
line plus the SECOM board row are now the two places where the "zero is ten" rule is
pinned by tests. The current note only re-checks that the pack lists them
(`Field Cipher Deep Dive — OD Message, VIC, Diana, Gronsfeld`) and that coverage stays at
zero dangling / zero orphaned entries. SECOM is the extended-checkerboard VIC variant
(the VIC article itself links to it as such), which is why the two are trained together.

## 4. Binary recipes

The kitchen already had `toHex`, `fromHex`, `toBinary`, `fromBinary`, `toASCII`,
`fromASCII`. What was missing for actual binary work was an offset-based dump and base-8
bytes:

* `toHexdump` / `fromHexdump` — 16-byte (or 8/32) lines with an eight-digit offset, two
  hex groups, and an ASCII gutter. `fromHexdump` reads only the offset and hex columns
  and **ignores the ASCII gutter**, so a corrupted or re-rendered gutter cannot inject
  bytes; the test feeds a poisoned gutter (`Hello 6f6f6f!`) and still recovers
  `Hello World!`.
* `toOctal` / `fromOctal` — space-separated octal bytes with three-digit padding or
  minimal width, and a reader that accepts three-digit groups, minimal values and the
  historical GNU/`od`-style tokens alike. Round trips are asserted against `toHex`.

Both dumps take the same `source` selector as the rest of the kitchen, so `Load File`
bytes (the raw byte string) can be dumped directly instead of being forced through UTF-8.

Two new packs make this reachable from the SFX "select all" installer:

* `Field Cipher Deep Dive — OD Message, VIC, Diana, Gronsfeld`
  (`odPoemExact`, `vicSchedule`, `vicExact`, `diana`, `dianaTable`, `gronsfeld`,
  `gronsfeldAnalyze`, `odPoemKey`, `secomExact`, `straddlingCheckerboard`)
* `Binary Bytes Lab — Hexdump & Octal`
  (`toHexdump`, `fromHexdump`, `toOctal`, `fromOctal`, `toHex`, `fromHex`, `toBinary`,
  `fromBinary`)

## 6. Open threads for continued research

0. **archive.org is now searched (2026-10-14).** Results, so the channel is not re-tread:
   the VIC-cipher "collection" there (`fav-vic_cipher`) is one user's bookmark folder whose
   only member is a 1940s-50s radio workbook (`workbookforradio0010bush`, 294 pages,
   borrow-only text layer), and the two items titled "poem code" (`poem-code-1`,
   `poem-code-2`, 2009) are Sharon Hopkins' *Perl poems* — `listen (a perl poem)`, not cipher
   material. Kahn's 1960 booklet *Two Soviet Spy Cyphers* and its 1993 reissue *Number One
   From Moscow* are not on archive.org (the CIA copy at cryptome remains the source used in
   §2b). The one real find is FM 31-20 (1965) above. Leads still open there: FM 31-20 (1971
   edition, `FM_31_20_S_F_O_T_1971`) for a later communications chapter, and STP 31-18E34-SM-TG
   (2003) for how the peacetime training pipeline described cryptography.

1. **Diana pad provenance — partly closed.** The layout and generation are now documented
   (matched tablet pair, five random five-letter groups per line, 30 groups per page,
   first two groups of the line sent in clear as the indicator, one page per A-Team). What
   is still missing is any surviving pad *book* scan: the letter alphabet used (24 letters
   with `Z` as operator/fill, per the CADCRYS description, versus all 26), and whether the
   page's group order carried any structure. A scan would let the recipe emit a realistic
   pad page.
2. **Diana + transposition variant.** zappi-tkn/Diana chains a columnar transposition
   after the trigraph step. If a published worked example surfaces, it can be added as a
   `mode` on `diana` rather than a new operation.
3. **VIC: the Russian (historical) checkerboard.** The hollow-nickel message is now driven
   through the schedule and both transpositions (§2c); what is still missing is the 40-cell,
   four-row Cyrillic board with its signs (`п/л`, `н/ц`, `н/т`, `пвт`, comma, full stop) and the
   "message starts" marker that the bisection uses in the Russian variant. Implementing it would
   let the kitchen encipher/decipher the nickel message end to end.
4. **VIC message-stage variants.** The Wikipedia example exercises one cut point and no
   keygroup; the recipe now covers transpositions and keygroup insertion and round-trips
   them, but a published vector with both would pin the defaults even harder.
5. **Gronsfeld history.** The cipher is usually attributed to Count Gronsfeld (17th c.);
   a primary source for the digit-key convention would let the recipe cite it instead of
   the generic Vigenère relationship.
6. **SECOM/Crow.** The 600-digit intercept still has no published plaintext; the bounds
   tool (`tools/crow_letter_count_bounds.py`) is the standing falsifier for any new
   letter-count claim, and `tools/secom_probe.py` re-checks the 105-digit example.
