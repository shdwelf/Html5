# Project Y — site and nuclear-research exhibits

Research checked: **30 September 2026**.

Open `los-alamos.html#sites`. The exhibit covers six selected, sourced, schematic sites: **Los Alamos**, **Oak Ridge**, **Hanford**, **Trinity**, **Operation Ivy / Mike**, and **Castle Bravo**. The first four are Manhattan Project-era sites; Ivy Mike and Castle Bravo extend the exhibit into postwar thermonuclear research and testing because the user requested continuation through Operation Ivy Mike and a correction of the “accidentally bigger explosion” thread.

The badge collection remains specifically a Los Alamos collection. Site history does **not** establish that any individual badge holder visited Oak Ridge, Hanford, Trinity, Enewetak, or Bikini, and the interface does not infer travel, clearances, assignments, identities, or badge numbers from these site models.

## Evidence-first correction: Ivy Mike, Castle Bravo, and Silly Putty

- **Ivy Mike** was the first full-scale thermonuclear test. DOE states that the Atomic Energy Commission detonated code-named “Mike” at Enewetak Atoll and gives the yield as **10.4 megatons**. [1](https://www.energy.gov/management/october-31-1952-mike-test)
- Britannica summarizes Operation Ivy / Mike as a thermonuclear test at Enewetak with a **10.4 Mt** yield and describes the crater as about **1,900 m** in diameter and **50 m** deep. [2](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Operation-Ivy)
- The famous unexpectedly larger-yield story is **Castle Bravo** in 1954, not Ivy Mike. Brookings summarizes Bravo as a 15 Mt test whose designers expected roughly five to six megatons; it attributes the miscalculation to underestimated reactions in the lithium-deuteride fusion fuel. [3](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/castle-bravo-the-largest-u-s-nuclear-explosion/)
- “Silly Putty” is **not** used in this exhibit as a causal explanation for a nuclear-test yield. It belongs to a separate wartime synthetic-rubber / silicone-polymer serendipity story unless a reliable source is provided connecting it to a specific nuclear-test claim.

The yield control is therefore labeled as a **relative comparison ring**. It supports variable-yield exploration only in the mathematical sense of scaling an illustrative ring by cube-root yield. It is not a weapon-design explainer, effects model, blast radius, fireball, fallout plume, dose, casualty, safety, or targeting calculator.

## 1. Los Alamos — research and development

Los Alamos was established as Project Y in 1943 and brought together the Manhattan Project’s scientific and weapon-development work. LANL’s institutional history describes this wartime origin. [4](https://www.lanl.gov/media/publications/national-security-science/0423-los-alamos-national-laboratory)

The NPS account explains that creation of the laboratory displaced Pueblo people and Anglo and Hispanic homesteaders, and repurposed the Los Alamos Ranch School. The exhibit does not describe the site as previously uninhabited. [5](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/-h-our-history-lesson-the-development-of-the-manhattan-project-in-los-alamos-county-new-mexico-wwii-heritage-city.htm)

**Model:** a symbolic mesa with representative technical, residential, and community blocks. These are not individually identified historic buildings. No footprint, street, terrain elevation, or placement was derived from a survey. It is an interpretive town vignette, not the modern laboratory or an authenticated 1943 plan.

## 2. Oak Ridge — uranium enrichment and pilot plutonium work

DOE Oak Ridge sources describe Site X / Clinton Engineer Works, the fenced wartime reservation, uranium-enrichment plants at **Y-12**, **K-25**, and **S-50**, and the **X-10 Graphite Reactor** pilot plant. [6](https://www.energy.gov/orem/history) [7](https://www.energy.gov/management/x-10-graphite-reactor) [8](https://www.energy.gov/em/oak-ridge)

The Atomic Heritage Foundation / Nuclear Museum summary identifies Oak Ridge as a major Manhattan Project location and gives public context for the rapid wartime city and industrial reservation. [9](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/location/oak-ridge-tn/)

**Model:** symbolic Tennessee ridges, a town block, Y-12, K-25, S-50, X-10, and a Clinch River strip. Footprints, distances, valley shapes, river placement, building heights, and alignments are invented. No calutrons, gaseous-diffusion cascades, reactor internals, chemical-separation details, modern cleanup geometry, or security-sensitive present-day geometry is modeled.

## 3. Hanford — plutonium production

The NPS identifies B Reactor as the world’s first full-scale nuclear-production reactor. Construction began in October 1943; criticality was achieved on **September 26, 1944**. The plutonium-production history connects Hanford with both Trinity and the bomb used at Nagasaki. [10](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/hanford-b-reactor-panoramic-tour.htm)

DOE’s historical overview places the wartime B, D, and F reactors along the Columbia River. This exhibit focuses only on a representative **B Reactor exterior**, not all reactor areas or the entire reservation. [11](https://www.energy.gov/management/b-reactor)

Nuclear Princeton documents the forced removal of Indigenous and non-Indigenous residents and the relationships of the Wanapum, Yakama, Umatilla, and Nez Perce peoples to the land. This context is included alongside the industrial history. [12](https://nuclearprinceton.princeton.edu/hanford-site)

**Model:** a generic reactor-building mass, annex, stack, support building, road, and river strip. Exterior proportions, dimensions, placement, and river course are invented. No core, fuel, cooling circuit, separation equipment, interior, operating controls, or present-day infrastructure is modeled. It must not be used as a facility blueprint or navigation aid.

## 4. Trinity — the first nuclear test

DOE dates the first nuclear explosion to **July 16, 1945**, on the Alamogordo Bombing Range in the Jornada del Muerto. Its account gives a **100-foot tower** and places the McDonald ranch house about **two miles south** of ground zero. [13](https://www.energy.gov/lm/trinity-site-worlds-first-nuclear-explosion)

The exhibit uses the date but avoids false precision about detonation time or yield. Sources may use rounded times and differing yield estimates. No time/energy simulation is implemented.

The National Cancer Institute’s community summary describes fallout-exposure reconstruction, projected health consequences, and substantial uncertainty. These are population-level projections, not a basis for diagnosing any individual. The exhibit links to that research rather than reducing Trinity to an isolated technical spectacle. [14](https://dceg.cancer.gov/research/how-we-study/exposure-assessment/trinity/community-summary)

**Model:** a symbolic pre-test tower and terrain, with a separately interpreted ranch-house block. The tower is 30.48 nominal metre-units high, corresponding to the sourced 100-foot height. All other dimensions—including platform, leg spacing, bracing, ranch-house form, and ground plane—are illustrative. The ranch house is compressed into a **displaced inset**, not located at its real separation from the tower. The ground-zero tile is an editorial marker, not a historical foundation, fallout boundary, crater, or the later obelisk. There is no device, internal assembly, blast animation, dose model, or fallout model.

## 5. Operation Ivy / Mike — thermonuclear transition

DOE’s short historical page states that the Atomic Energy Commission detonated the first thermonuclear device, code-named **Mike**, at Enewetak Atoll and gives the yield as **10.4 megatons**. [1](https://www.energy.gov/management/october-31-1952-mike-test)

Britannica’s Operation Ivy summary provides an independent general-reference check for date/location/yield and crater scale. [2](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Operation-Ivy)

**Model:** a symbolic atoll and lagoon scene with Elugelab, a crater marker, support island, and a generic shot-cab block. It does not depict the cryogenic liquid-deuterium device, weapon internals, cab engineering, blast, fallout, or actual bathymetry. The Enewetak terrain is represented as a flat atoll fallback because USGS 3DEP does not cover the Marshall Islands in the same way as U.S. terrain tiles.

## 6. Castle Bravo — correction/comparison for the unexpected-yield story

Castle Bravo is included because it is the correct historical home of the unexpectedly large thermonuclear-yield story. Brookings summarizes the test as the largest U.S. nuclear explosion, with a roughly 15 Mt actual yield compared with an expected five to six megatons, and describes serious fallout consequences. [3](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/castle-bravo-the-largest-u-s-nuclear-explosion/)

The exhibit presents Bravo as a caution label beside Ivy Mike, not as a device-design lesson. It does not reveal or model weapon design details. Its “unexpected yield” text remains at the publicly documented, high-level historical level: a mistaken assumption about lithium-deuteride fuel reactions, not Silly Putty.

**Model:** a symbolic Bikini Atoll scene with reef, shot area, crater marker, and an interpretive fallout-direction cue. It is not a plume reconstruction, fallout deposition map, surveyed crater model, or bathymetric map.

## Minimal DEM-style overlay

The canvas under the 3D viewer uses a low-resolution, offline **DEM-style overlay** to place each site on a simple terrain background:

- Los Alamos: mesa relief sketch.
- Oak Ridge: ridge-and-valley sketch.
- Hanford: Columbia Plateau / river-strip sketch.
- Trinity: basin sketch.
- Enewetak and Bikini: flat atoll fallback.

This is deliberately minimal. It is labeled as “USGS DEM minimal overlay” to keep the workflow compatible with the existing project style and with USGS 3DEP usage elsewhere in this repository. The site page draws an offline terrain sketch first and includes an optional **Sample live USGS 3DEP point** action for the four U.S. sites; if CORS/network access fails, the page keeps the offline overlay and says so. It does not ship survey-quality raster elevation data. For Pacific atolls, the overlay is explicitly a fallback because standard USGS 3DEP U.S. terrain coverage is not a clean source for those locations, and Ivy Mike crater bathymetry would require separate authoritative bathymetric sourcing.

## 4Dwm, Three.js, and VRML implementation

- Viewer: existing locally vendored **Three.js r170** and `OrbitControls.js`; no CDN or new production dependency.
- Shared model data: `js/project-y-sites-data.js` contains interpretive box/beam primitives, yield presets, DEM-overlay labels, and research metadata.
- Display: `js/project-y-sites.js` builds a Three.js mesh for each primitive. Rendering occurs on view changes, rather than in a perpetual animation loop. WebGL initialization is deferred until the section is near the viewport.
- 4Dwm: `los-alamos.html#sites` has buttons for “Los Alamos site,” “All represented sites,” and “Pip current site.” These dispatch site-window events into the existing 4Dwm manager so site notes can float beside badge windows.
- Controls: orbit, pan, zoom, reset, top view, rotation buttons, wireframe, site tabs, yield preset buttons, and yield slider. Buttons provide alternatives to mouse/touch gestures.
- Accessibility/failure mode: historical descriptions, sources, and download links remain available without WebGL. The `.wrl` files are also directly linked in the HTML for users without JavaScript.
- Exports: `models/project-y/los-alamos.wrl`, `oak-ridge.wrl`, `hanford.wrl`, `trinity.wrl`, `ivy-mike.wrl`, and `castle-bravo.wrl` are **VRML 2.0 UTF-8** scenes using `Transform`, `Shape`, `Box`, `Appearance`, `Material`, `Viewpoint`, and lighting nodes. Source links and model caveats travel with the files in `WorldInfo`.
- VRML matches the solid exterior model. Wireframe is a browser-view option, not a different export. Historical notes and landmark descriptions appear in HTML; they are not 3D text labels.
- These `.wrl` files are intended for a separate VRML-compatible viewer. This is not an immersive WebXR headset mode, VRML importer, or VRML browser plugin.

### Offline webxdc packages

`los-alamos.xdc` is the focused Project Y distribution. It carries the archive
page, the shared site data and renderer, every one of the six generated VRML
files, local Three.js modules, local imagery, and the source notes. `sitek.xdc`
contains that same current Project Y page and dependency tree alongside the
SITE-K shell. Rebuild the complete set with:

```sh
sh tools/pack_xdc.sh
```

The latter also regenerates the focused Project Y package, so the standalone
and SITE-K copies cannot silently diverge when a new represented site is added.

### Validate

```sh
node --test tests/los-alamos.test.mjs tests/project-y-sites.test.mjs
python scripts/serve-los-alamos.py
# In another terminal, with optional browser-test packages installed:
node tests/project-y-sites-browser.mjs
```

The data tests verify valid finite primitive transforms, citation metadata, geometry-count parity, exact agreement between generated and committed VRML, explicit placement limits, and the Ivy/Bravo correction. Browser tests exercise real Chromium/SwiftShader rendering, site switches, view controls, downloadable file responses, mobile overflow, and simulated WebGL failure. They do not certify historical survey accuracy, blast effects, fallout effects, or compatibility with every third-party VRML viewer.
