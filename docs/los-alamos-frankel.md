# Stanley P. Frankel — badge O-2

Research checked: 14 September 2026. Seventh curated record in the Project Y app.

## Identification and two different photographs

The higher-resolution Commons file identifies its sitter as **Stanley Frankel**, credits **Los Alamos National Laboratory**, and gives only **c. 1940s** as its date. Its visible identifier is read here as **O 2**, normalized to **O-2** using the letter O. This is a visual reading, not a transcription from an issuance register. The exact exposure date, badge issue date, and clearance remain unestablished. [1](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Frankel-stanley_p.jpg)

A separate Commons file, **Stanley P. Frankel Los Alamos ID.png**, is a 130 × 180-pixel wartime badge portrait. It shows a different pose, clothing, and visible identifier. The markings in that small image are not confidently transcribed in this update. The images must not be presented as merely different resolutions of the same exposure, nor does their existence establish which badge came first. Upload dates (2012 and 2023) are not exposure dates. [1](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Frankel-stanley_p.jpg) [2](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Stanley_P._Frankel_Los_Alamos_ID.png)

The app uses the higher-resolution portrait as `assets/los-alamos/frankel.jpg`, with the canonical Commons filename `Frankel-stanley p.jpg` as its record ID. The alternate image is linked from the profile rather than assigned a guessed badge number.

## Wartime computing was a collective effort

The Nuclear Museum identifies Frankel as a former Oppenheimer physics student at Berkeley who later became a computer scientist. Its computing history places Frankel and Eldred Nelson at Los Alamos in **spring 1943**, helping organize human computing within the Theoretical Division and proposing Marchant desk calculators. [3](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/profile/stan-frankel/) [4](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/history/human-computers-los-alamos/)

The same history names **Donald “Moll” Flanders** as leader of T-5 and describes the women who performed its calculations, including Mary Frankel, Josephine Elliot, Beatrice Langer, Augusta “Mici” Teller, Jean Bacher, and Kay Manley. This distinction matters: “helped organize computing” should not become an unsupported claim that Frankel alone led every computing group or performed its work. [4](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/history/human-computers-los-alamos/)

## ENIAC: a documented wartime/postwar boundary

Nicholas Metropolis’s **1987 retrospective**, “The Beginning of the Monte Carlo Method,” supplies a more precise account than short biographical summaries. [5](https://mcnp.lanl.gov/pdf_files/Article_1987_LAS_Metropolis_125--130.pdf)

| Period | What the account actually says |
| --- | --- |
| March 1945 | Metropolis, Frankel, and John von Neumann visited the Moore School at the University of Pennsylvania for an advance look at ENIAC. [5](https://mcnp.lanl.gov/pdf_files/Article_1987_LAS_Metropolis_125--130.pdf) |
| End of the war | Their set of problems was not yet complete. They continued working, with Anthony Turkevich joining and contributing to the effort. [5](https://mcnp.lanl.gov/pdf_files/Article_1987_LAS_Metropolis_125--130.pdf) |
| Spring 1946 | Frankel, Turkevich, and Metropolis described ENIAC, calculations, and conclusions at a Los Alamos review. [5](https://mcnp.lanl.gov/pdf_files/Article_1987_LAS_Metropolis_125--130.pdf) |

The account identifies the work as an early thermonuclear computation. The app records the historical collaboration and dates only; it does not reproduce technical weapon calculations. Nor does it say that ENIAC produced the calculations for the wartime atomic bombs. The source distinguishes ENIAC from the earlier electromechanical punched-card equipment. [5](https://mcnp.lanl.gov/pdf_files/Article_1987_LAS_Metropolis_125--130.pdf)

### Evidence limits

- Metropolis’s article is participant testimony written decades later, not a contemporaneous travel log.
- The museum’s short Frankel biography says he learned about ENIAC after the war. That compressed wording omits the wartime visit described in Metropolis’s account. The app explicitly attributes the March 1945 date to Metropolis rather than silently treating all summaries as identical. [3](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/profile/stan-frankel/) [5](https://mcnp.lanl.gov/pdf_files/Article_1987_LAS_Metropolis_125--130.pdf)
- Later computer designs and commercial milestones need their own museum/catalog sources before inclusion. They should not be inferred from a wartime badge photograph.

## Next research leads

1. **Eldred Nelson:** the clearest next colleague to investigate, given his documented spring 1943 computing work alongside Frankel. Locate the badge photograph and independently transcribe its identifier. [4](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/history/human-computers-los-alamos/)
2. **Mary Frankel:** named as a human computer. Research her as a worker in her own right; do not infer duties solely from a family relationship. [4](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/history/human-computers-los-alamos/)
3. **Donald Flanders:** establish the T-5 leadership record and match it to an identified badge photograph. [4](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/history/human-computers-los-alamos/)
4. **Anthony Turkevich:** verify a badge and distinguish wartime service from the postwar ENIAC collaboration described by Metropolis. [5](https://mcnp.lanl.gov/pdf_files/Article_1987_LAS_Metropolis_125--130.pdf)
