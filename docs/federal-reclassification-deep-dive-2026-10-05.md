## 0. Scope & honesty note

This document reconstructs **how federal penal provisions read before and after a reclassification event**, using two anchors agreed for this session:

1. **Title 18 recodification** — the historical framework for how the United States has "recreated" its penal code when offenses get reclassified (1948 positive-law enactment; the Brown Commission; the Sentencing Reform Act offense-letter grades).

## 1. How the federal penal code gets "recreated": Title 18 recodification history

The United States has never had a European-style unitary "penal code." What it has is **Title 18, Crimes and Criminal Procedure**, plus penal provisions scattered across ~50 other titles (the CSA's penalties live in Title 21). Each time Congress has reorganized this corpus, offenses were literally *reclassified* — renumbered, regraded, merged, or repealed:

| Event | Instrument | What was reclassified |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Revised Statutes of 1874** | Act of June 22, 1874 | First consolidation of federal crimes (Title LXX, "Crimes") out of session laws. |
| **Criminal Code of 1909** | Act of March 4, 1909, ch. 321, **35 Stat. 1088** | Repealed and re-enacted the federal criminal corpus; offenses renumbered into a single code. |
| **1948 recodification** | Act of June 25, 1948, ch. 645, **62 Stat. 683** | Enacted Title 18 into **positive law**. Hundreds of sections renumbered (e.g., old § 338 mail fraud → **18 U.S.C. § 1341**). The reviser's notes in the Historical and Revision Notes remain the authoritative concordance for the old → new mapping. |
| **Brown Commission (1966–1971)** | National Commission on Reform of Federal Criminal Laws, *Final Report* (1971) | Proposed a complete regrading of all federal offenses into felony classes A–C, misdemeanors A–B, infraction — never enacted. |
| **S. 1 / S. 1437 era (1973–1982)** | S. 1437, 95th Cong. (passed Senate Jan. 30, 1978; died in House) | The closest Congress ever came to a comprehensive recodification; collapsed ~3,000 offenses into a graded structure. |
| **Sentencing Reform Act of 1984** | Pub. L. 98-473, Title II | The *partial* reclassification that stuck: **18 U.S.C. § 3559** now classifies every federal offense by maximum term into Classes A–E felonies, Class A–C misdemeanors, and infractions — a grading overlay rather than a renumbering. |

**The recodification lesson that matters for the worked example:** when Congress reclassifies, the *offense text* and the *grading overlay* are separate layers. A substance moving between CSA schedules changes the *classification layer* while most *offense text* stays put — which is exactly why the marijuana reclassification produces the counterintuitive penalty results in § 3 below.

### 1.1 The CSA as a penal mini-code

The Controlled Substances Act (Pub. L. 91-513, Title II, Oct. 27, 1970; **21 U.S.C. § 801 et seq.**) is itself a graded penal code keyed to five schedules:

- **§ 812(b)** — placement criteria (findings required per schedule);
- **§ 812(c)** — the initial schedules; marihuana at Schedule I(c)(10), THC at (c)(17);
- **§ 811(a)–(c)** — rulemaking transfer procedure, requiring the HHS scientific/medical evaluation and the **eight-factor analysis**;
- **§ 811(d)(1)** — the **treaty exception**: scheduling *by order*, without the § 811(a)–(c) findings and procedures, where international treaty obligations require control;
- **§§ 841–865** — the offense and penalty chapter keyed partly to schedule and partly to *named substances*.

## 3. The penal sections, recreated as they get reclassified

### 3.1 What the April 2026 order actually moved

Regulatory layer (21 C.F.R. Part 1308): the covered categories move from the **§ 1308.11 (Schedule I)** list to **§ 1308.13 (Schedule III)**. The *statutory* schedules in 21 U.S.C. § 812(c) are unamended — § 812(c) is only the initial 1970 snapshot; current placement lives in the regulations.

### 3.2 § 812(b): the findings layer

**Before (status quo for Schedule I placement):**

> § 812(b)(1) — Schedule I. (A) The drug … has a high potential for abuse. (B) The drug … has no currently accepted medical use in treatment in the United States. (C) There is a lack of accepted safety for use of the drug … under medical supervision.

**After (the finding the reclassification asserts):**

> § 812(b)(3) — Schedule III. (A) The drug … has a potential for abuse less than the drugs or other substances in schedules I and II. (B) The drug … has a currently accepted medical use in treatment in the United States. (C) Abuse of the drug … may lead to moderate or low physical dependence or high psychological dependence.

Note the April 2026 order's distinctive move: under § 811(d)(1) these findings are *not legally required* for the treaty-pathway order — the order recites the HHS findings but does not rest on them. That is simultaneously its administrative-law strength (no notice-and-comment needed) and its litigation vulnerability (see *NORML v. DEA*, 559 F.2d 735 (D.C. Cir. 1977), construing § 811(d) narrowly).

### 3.3 § 841: trafficking — the text that does NOT change

The core counterintuitive result, consistently flagged by CRS: the marijuana trafficking penalties in § 841(b) are keyed to **"marihuana" by name and quantity, not by schedule**. They read the same the day after full rescheduling:

> § 841(b)(1)(A)(vii) — 1,000 kg+ mixture / 1,000+ plants: **10 years to life**.
> § 841(b)(1)(B)(vii) — 100 kg+ / 100+ plants: **5 to 40 years**.
> § 841(b)(1)(D) — less than 50 kg (except ≥50 plants), 10 kg hashish, 1 kg hashish oil: **up to 5 years**.

Compare the *generic* Schedule III tier that would govern a substance identified only by schedule:

> § 841(b)(1)(E)(i) — in the case of a controlled substance in schedule III: **up to 10 years** (15 if death or serious bodily injury results).

So a reclassification to Schedule III produces a drafting anomaly: the generic Schedule III ceiling (10 years) is *higher* than the specific small-quantity marijuana ceiling (5 years). The orthodox reading (specific controls general) keeps §§ (A)(vii)/(B)(vii)/(D) operative; the point has not been definitively litigated. **What changes operationally is not the penalty text but the lawfulness of the underlying conduct**: a DEA-registered manufacturer/distributor/dispenser handling covered Schedule III marijuana within 21 U.S.C. §§ 822–823 and the prescription requirement of § 829(b) commits no § 841 offense at all.

### 3.4 § 844: simple possession — where the schedule does bite

> § 844(a) — It shall be unlawful for any person knowingly or intentionally to possess a controlled substance **unless such substance was obtained directly, or pursuant to a valid prescription or order, from a practitioner** acting in the course of his professional practice …

Under Schedule I there can be no valid prescription (no accepted medical use), so *every* possession is an offense. Under Schedule III, possession **pursuant to a valid prescription is lawful** — the prescription exception finally has something to attach to. This is the clearest example of penal text whose *meaning* is reclassified while its *words* are untouched.

### 3.5 Collateral penal and quasi-penal provisions

| Provision | Before (Sched. I) | After (Sched. III, covered categories) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **26 U.S.C. § 280E** (no deductions for trafficking in Sched. I/II) | Applies — effective tax rates often >70% | **Does not apply** to Schedule III activity; IRS/Treasury clarification in progress post-April order |
| **18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(3)** (gun possession by unlawful user) | Applies | **Still applies** — turns on "unlawful user of any controlled substance," not schedule |
| **8 U.S.C. § 1182(a)(2)** (inadmissibility) | Applies | Still applies — keyed to any controlled-substance offense |
| **21 U.S.C. § 862 / § 881** (benefit denial / forfeiture) | Apply | Apply where conduct remains an offense |
| **46 U.S.C. § 70503** (maritime) | Applies | Applies — schedule-neutral |
| Research registration (21 U.S.C. § 823(f)) | Schedule I protocol burdens | Schedule III registration; April order lets registered researchers source from state-licensed entities |

### 3.6 The "hybrid schedule" objection

The April order retains Schedule I/II-style controls (quotas, import/export permits, enhanced registration) on the rescheduled categories to maintain Single Convention compliance. Petitioners in the D.C. Circuit argue Congress authorized no such hybrid; the government answers that § 811(d)(1) commands the AG to select the schedule *and* controls needed "to carry out such obligations." This is the sharpest purely legal question in the case — formalized as Argument C in the symbolic-logic companion.

## 5. Where this goes next

Three decision points control the endgame:

1. **Oct. 13, 2026** — DEA's response to the GAO-report motion; the ALJ then lifts or extends the stay and (eventually) issues a recommended decision to Administrator Terry Cole, followed by exceptions and the Administrator's final rule.
2. **D.C. Circuit merits** on the April 2026 order — the § 811(d)(1) scope question under *NORML* and the hybrid-schedule question. A vacatur would re-split the two tracks.
3. **Congress** — any of the catalogued bills (see catalog file) could moot the administrative track in either direction; the appropriations riders remain the only enacted congressional position.
