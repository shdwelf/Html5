## 1. Recommendation: 1Password, with explicit qualifications

For the selected **best overall**, paid, general-purpose Android + browser + desktop use case, choose **1Password**. This is a product-fit recommendation based on its documented account-password/Secret Key design, cross-platform client model, sharing and recovery workflows—not an experimentally measured usability score, an assertion of cryptographic superiority, or a promise of malicious-server immunity. Review the [security design](https://1password.com/security) and trial the current signed clients with dummy logins on the owner's actual devices before migrating.

| Priority | Shortlist | Reason / trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Best overall, willing to pay | **1Password** | Broad documented cross-platform workflow and dual-key design; proprietary hosted service, paid plans, no self-hosted personal vault. Some audit reports require Trust Center access. The primary research **does report attacks on it**. |
| Open source / value / free tier | **Bitwarden** | Public client/server projects, published assessment links, managed or official self-hosted options. Exact component licenses, deployment/recovery and current remediation still matter. |
| Direct local file control | **KeePassXC**, plus separately assessed **KeePassDX** or KeePass2Android | Portable encrypted KDBX files and desktop/browser workflows; the owner operates synchronization, conflicts, backups and recovery. The Android client is a separate project, not a mobile build of KeePassXC. |
| Already using Proton, aliases important | **Proton Pass** | Open-source clients and an alias/privacy ecosystem. Check current recovery, export and passkey portability; absence from a particular paper is not an audit pass. |
| Teams that deliberately operate infrastructure | Passbolt / Psono, or official Bitwarden deployment | Evaluate administration, permissions, patching, TLS, client distribution and restore drills. “Self-hosted” is not inherently more secure. |

No account was created, paid for or migrated. No real vault, master password, recovery phrase, private key or browser credential database was requested or tested. Prices and live plan entitlements are deliberately not guessed. Regional/device accessibility, the current extension/app UX and export fidelity remain owner trials.

### What would change this recommendation?

A strict open-source requirement or subscription rejection favors Bitwarden; a requirement to avoid a hosted vault service favors the independently managed KeePass ecosystem. An organization may prioritize administrative isolation/compliance over personal convenience. A compromised unlocked endpoint, malicious extension/client update, weak recovery channel or mishandled plaintext export can defeat any choice. These are not solved by picking the largest cipher/key-size label.

## 2. Primary security research: do not misread “zero knowledge”

The [full version of IACR 2026/058](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/058), associated with USENIX Security 2026, was received January 14 and its metadata lists an **August 12, 2026** revision. All **20 returned text chunks** of the [PDF](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/058.pdf) were read in this investigation. That is literature coverage, **not** reproduction of its proofs of concept, a binary/source audit of current releases, or an independent remediation retest.

Its powerful threat model gives an attacker control of the service/server protocol, while excluding the trivial case of simply replacing the distributed client with openly malicious code. That exclusion does not make mutable web-client delivery safe in real life. The paper considers key distribution, recovery/sharing, vault integrity/metadata, legacy/downgrade and related protocol behavior. Its reported attack counts are:

| Studied product | Reported attacks in the full paper | Interpretation |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Bitwarden | 12 | Not “12 currently exploitable vulnerabilities in every version.” |
| LastPass | 7 | Not proof that every account or password was decrypted. |
| Dashlane | 6 | Version, legacy-data and workflow distinctions matter. |
| 1Password | 6 | **Not zero attacks**, notwithstanding differing vendor framing. |

Counts depend on study scope and are **not a security ranking**. They do not justify ranking unstudied managers as safer. In particular, 1Password's additional Secret Key improves resistance to password guessing against stolen encrypted data, but does **not** authenticate a malicious server's recovery/sharing public-key directory or automatically prevent vault substitution. MFA can harden account login; it is not a proof against the paper's server-controlled protocol attacks.

### Vendor responses: scope and time matter

- [1Password response](https://1password.com/blog/eth-zurich-zero-knowledge-malicious-server-review), February 16: acknowledges server-mediated public-key distribution / related design limitations and characterizes them as known rather than newly discovered. **“No new attack vectors” is not “no attacks.”** This response predates the August full revision.
- [Bitwarden response](https://bitwarden.com/blog/security-through-transparency-eth-zurich-audits-bitwarden-cryptography/): its “addressed” framing includes **seven resolved or in active remediation** and **three accepted design decisions**. That is not equivalent to every issue patched and independently retested; findings and paper attack counts also need not map one-to-one.
- [Dashlane response](https://www.dashlane.com/blog/zero-knowledge-malicious-server): reports a legacy CBC fix in extension **6.2544.1, November 5, 2025**, and separately discusses authenticated sharing keys / transaction consistency. A dated fix in one extension is not certification of every current platform or protocol property.
- [LastPass hardening response](https://blog.lastpass.com/posts/details-on-hardening-in-response-to-eth-zurich-reported-security-issues): distinguishes completed icon mitigation, a planned March 2026 migration and longer-term recovery/sharing/integrity work. This review did **not** independently verify all planned work completed; a scheduled date is not delivery evidence.

The separate [Bitwarden npm CLI supply-chain statement](https://community.bitwarden.com/t/bitwarden-statement-on-checkmarx-supply-chain-incident/96127) concerns an April 2026 CLI package/host-environment incident. It must not be silently turned into a demonstrated breach of encrypted stored vaults, or ignored as irrelevant to how software is installed. None of these vendor statements substitutes for a current independent retest.

### Other audit evidence is version/configuration scoped

- [KeePassXC's audit index](https://keepassxc.org/audits/) lists the January 19, 2023 source review of **2.7.4** and **ANSSI-CSPN-2025/16** for **2.7.9** in a specified Windows configuration. The [certificate](https://messervices.cyber.gouv.fr/visas/ANSSI-CSPN-2025-16-certificat.pdf) expressly disclaims a blanket recommendation or guarantee of no exploitable flaws. The [security target](https://messervices.cyber.gouv.fr/visas/ANSSI-CSPN-2025-16-cible.pdf) was read in all three returned chunks: particular database, browser-integration, environment and authentication assumptions apply. It is not a certificate for KeePassDX, all current releases, every OS or every plugin/sync provider. Only the first returned chunk of the older 2023 technical review was inspected here; the full Synacktiv technical evaluation was not independently reproduced.
- [Proton Pass's historical Cure53 announcement](https://proton.me/blog/pass-open-source-security-audit) links source and a 2023 assessment, including an Android platform-related issue it says could not then be resolved. That historic evidence is not a claim that every current Android/client workflow is vulnerability-free. The four-manager paper did not study Proton Pass.
- Public source, a compliance logo, a chosen primitive, an “audited” badge or a high star count alone does not establish end-to-end product security. Review the exact report, version, platform, configuration and outstanding findings.

## 4. “Web based torrent” is not one architecture

| Category | Examples in the 27-source directory | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open-content / publisher distribution | Internet Archive, Academic Torrents, FOSS Torrents, Debian, Ubuntu | Prefer item licenses and independently authenticated publisher signatures/checksums. A torrent hash alone is not publisher identity. |
| Public metadata/search index | BTDigg, Knaben | Untrusted indexed claims. No seed-count, result safety, rights, operator or current API/CORS certification was performed. |
| Self-hosted indexer / adapter | Bitmagnet, Prowlarr, Jackett, Magnetissimo, Rats | A backend/crawler/database and operational security are required. DHT crawling is network participation. Atlas does not deploy one. |
| Historical self-hosted project | Magnetico | Archived reference, not a fresh deployment recommendation. |
| Browser peer client | WebTorrent, Instant.io, βTorrent | **Not search engines.** Browser WebTorrent uses WebRTC peers, not arbitrary native TCP/UDP swarms; bridges/web seeds/compatible peers matter. |
| Server-to-browser gateway | Webtor | Its server handles peer communication and learns the supplied magnet/torrent and delivery requests. “In browser” is not local-only or anonymous. |
| Remote UI for native backend | Flood, qBittorrent Web UI, Transmission Web UI | Still requires a running authorized native client/backend. Keep admin/RPC private; plugins can be executable code. |
| Directory / endpoint list | Shadow Libraries, TrackersList, searx.space in its own role | Not a payload search/client. More trackers disclose a hash/IP to more operators; they do not authenticate content. |

For legitimate source discovery, start with the publisher/open-content catalogs. For an owner-operated indexing project, compare Bitmagnet's DHT index model against Prowlarr/Jackett's configured-source adapter model; neither is installed or anonymously operated by adding a web UI. The [WebTorrent FAQ](https://webtorrent.io/faq), [Webtor primary site](https://webtor.io/) and pinned upstream observations support these distinctions. Site names in search results were not promoted to trusted installers or invisible app APIs.

## 5. Torrent Atlas: implemented browser application

Atlas is an original, local-first workbench with **27 typed sources, seven outbound search templates and a 37-row password-manager/client/retired-feature comparison**. It is **not** a live result aggregator or torrent downloader. The deterministic builder copies exactly eight runtime files into `public/apps/torrent-atlas/`; no CDN, extra data dump or unreviewed source tree is copied. It is integrated into public/network catalogs, not the fixed private or microphone allowlists.

Implemented tools:

1. **Discover:** local filtering and consent → exact URL preview → separate open/copy. Input changes/clear invalidate a prepared link. No secret query or account credentials belong here.
2. **Magnet lab:** v1 hex/base32, SHA-256 v2 multihash and hybrid **claims**. Invalid encodings, duplicate/conflicting topics and unsupported hashes fail closed. Minimal copy drops trackers, web seeds, explicit peers, file selections and unknown parameters. No handler is invoked and no metadata/peer lookup occurs. A minimal magnet can lose discovery information and never makes a later download anonymous; do not use it to redistribute a private torrent.
3. **Inspect:** strict canonical bencode with original `info` byte slices for SHA-1/SHA-256; name/path/collision/length/private checks; optional complete expected-hash comparison; v2 piece-layer/Merkle-root consistency and hybrid file/padding checks. Endpoints/paths render as text, are never fetched, extracted or executed. A private flag suppresses magnet export.
4. **Create:** deterministic **v1** metainfo for 1–256 explicitly selected flat files, up to 256 MiB; pieces span file boundaries. Private mode requires an explicit tracker and suppresses magnet export. Filenames/sizes are disclosed by metadata; the app neither uploads nor seeds files.
5. **Save:** explicit browser download is labeled a request, not read-back proof. If the existing Android `Html5LocalFiles` v1 adapter is present, validate completed byte count and compare its SHA-256 receipt to the created torrent bytes. Cancel/error/mismatched receipts remain failures, not saves. Native picker/export permission/origin bounds are unchanged; browser adapter doubles are **not owner-device evidence**.

Protocol references read: [BEP 3](https://www.bittorrent.org/beps/bep_0003.html), [BEP 9](https://www.bittorrent.org/beps/bep_0009.html), [BEP 52](https://www.bittorrent.org/beps/bep_0052.html) (all four returned sections), [BEP 53](https://www.bittorrent.org/beps/bep_0053.html). Peer metadata exchange is a different operation and is **not implemented**. SHA-1 is retained for legacy BitTorrent identifiers, not cryptographic authentication.

### Bounds, lifecycle and deliberate exclusions

- Metadata ≤4 MiB, nesting ≤32, nodes/pieces ≤65,536, files ≤4,096; magnet ≤16,384 characters / 128 parameters; tracker/web-seed entries ≤32 per type.
- Creation ≤256 MiB / 256 files, power-of-two pieces 16 KiB–4 MiB. Source files are read in piece-sized slices; metadata remains bounded.
- Inspect/create use a new local module worker with a hard **60-second** limit. Input edit, Stop, tool switch, Clear and page-hide terminate work/discard stale replies. No main-thread fallback.
- No runtime `fetch`, XHR, socket, WebRTC peer, tracker, DHT, service worker, analytics or app history storage. CSP blocks connections/remote execution. Static source checks and intercepted-request browser controls supplement, not prove, this boundary.
- No v2/hybrid **creation**, directory traversal/extraction, arbitrary non-UTF-8 repair, ambiguous unordered dictionaries, symlinks, resume/seeding, remote metadata resolution, hidden proxy or search-API aggregation. Conservative filename/case/normalization rules deliberately reject some otherwise usable torrents.

A matching infohash proves neither the payload, publisher/signature, malware absence, rights nor seed availability. Self-consistent v2 metadata is not an independent payload verification. A browser/origin/extension compromise is outside a local-code privacy promise. Ordinary BitTorrent should not be routed through Tor; see the [Tor Project warning](https://support.torproject.org/about-tor/using-and-sharing/torrent/). Nothing in Atlas activates the gateway planner or supplies anonymity.

### Executed independent controls

`tools/torrent-atlas-interop.py` uses a hash-pinned **libtorrent 2.0.11** CPython 3.11 Linux x86_64 test wheel, not a runtime dependency. It constructs **no libtorrent session**:

- Generates **18 deterministic independent fixtures**: single/small/multi-file × 16/64 KiB pieces × v1/v2/hybrid, with creation dates removed. Atlas checks hashes, sizes and non-padding file counts against them.
- libtorrent parses **four Atlas exports**: single-file, cross-file pieces, private tracker and Unicode filename. It compares infohash, size and private status. This is independent metadata interoperability, **not** a download/seed test or exhaustive rehash of arbitrary real payloads.
- **Zero peer sessions**. No trackers, DHT, index API or swarm was contacted by these controls. Unit tests separately check known piece hashes, malformed layers, private export refusal and cancellation.

The browser suites use real Chromium/module workers and synthetic files, with external requests intercepted. Final counts, full regression, Java parity, exact-head CI and remaining Android/device gaps are in round 95; overlapping test totals are not summed into a misleading overall count.

## 6. OpenPGP follow-up: alternative message inputs

The user clarified **encrypted messages/files**, not bypassing private-key cryptography. [OpenPGP Workbench](../apps/openpgp-workbench/README.md) now accepts pasted armor or explicitly chosen armored/binary message files for one passphrase decryption attempt. Password-recipient messages do not require a private key. Public-key-only ciphertext still requires its matching private key / authorized hardware key in a trusted client; the workflow explains that rather than asking for an unrelated substitute or weakening keyring/recovery rules.

Input ≤2 MiB, output ≤1 MiB, at most four password recipients, 64 MiB Argon2 cap and a terminating 30-second local worker. UTF-8 is inert text; binary payloads have a separate acknowledgement/save as fixed `.bin`, never automatic execution/extraction. Independent GnuPG control messages, exact binary bytes, wrong-password/key-required failures, worker lifecycle and Chromium file/save controls are tested. Keybase opt-in discovery, exact-fingerprint gates, public-only persistence and the existing native file policy remain separate and unchanged.

## 7. Before adopting any password manager

Install current signed official clients/extensions; use a unique long random master passphrase; keep recovery materials/Secret Key in a secure independent place; register two phishing-resistant hardware keys where supported; avoid a circular recovery dependency on a locked vault. Trial full-domain matching/autofill, multi-device recovery and emergency access with dummy accounts. Check attachment/passkey/export portability. Test an encrypted backup/restore before deleting the old vault. CSV and many migration exports are **plaintext**; local handling and deletion do not guarantee secure erasure. Do not place real vaults/private keys into Atlas, metasearch, chat, torrents or an online recovery site.

The original backlog remains **31 terminal tasks: 30 done, T9.3 declined**. Research coverage, a metadata workbench, a planner build and one ISO acquisition do not close real VPN packet-path, owner-device, independent key-trust, account/migration, private-backup or financial/publication gates.
