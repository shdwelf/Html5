# Nicholas Metropolis — badge G-15

Research update: 14 September 2026. Added to the Project Y app as the sixth curated record.

## Badge identification

- **Sitter:** Nicholas Metropolis, identified by the Wikimedia Commons file description and independently represented in the Nuclear Museum biography. [1](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Metropolis-nicholas.jpg) [2](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/profile/nicholas-metropolis/)
- **Visible identifier:** **G 15**, directly transcribed from the photograph; the app normalizes its typography to **G-15**. This is a visual transcription, not a finding in a badge-issue ledger. [1](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Metropolis-nicholas.jpg)
- **Image date:** the file description says **c. 1940s**. Do not turn the archive-wide c. 1943–1947 range into an exact date for this exposure. [1](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Metropolis-nicholas.jpg)
- **Credit:** Los Alamos National Laboratory, via Wikimedia Commons. The file carries an attribution license and a required notice, reproduced in `assets/los-alamos/NOTICE-metropolis.txt`. It is not labeled here as unrestricted public-domain material. [1](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Metropolis-nicholas.jpg)
- **Not established:** badge issue date, meaning of the letter G, clearance/color, replacement-badge history, or employee arrival sequence. Nothing in the sources reviewed justifies inferring these from the number or monochrome portrait.

## Wartime role: keep this in the Manhattan Project story

Metropolis earned his physics doctorate at the University of Chicago in 1941. Oppenheimer recruited him to Los Alamos in 1943. The Nuclear Museum biography describes his work with Fermi and Teller on equations for material behavior at high temperatures and pressures. [2](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/profile/nicholas-metropolis/)

His interview with Richard Rhodes, dated **12 September 1993**, confirms his arrival in 1943. In the transcript, Metropolis recalls repairing mechanical tabletop calculators with Richard Feynman rather than shipping them away, then adapting IBM business machines to scientific calculations. LANL's institutional history also credits him with helping assemble and operate wartime IBM punched-card equipment. [3](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/voices/oral-histories/nicholas-metropolis-interview/) [4](https://www.lanl.gov/media/publications/the-vault/0822-metropolis)

### Source criticism

The museum biography calls the Marchant machines “analog” computers; Metropolis's transcript calls them **mechanical** computers. The app uses “mechanical desk calculators,” avoiding the misleading analog classification. The biography and interview also differ about who proposed the human-versus-machine comparison. The app attributes neither origin story as settled fact. [2](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/profile/nicholas-metropolis/) [3](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/voices/oral-histories/nicholas-metropolis-interview/)

The oral-history introduction blends wartime arrival with later computing developments. For chronology, distinguish the interview transcript from its editorial summary and corroborate with dated institutional histories. A late-life recollection is valuable primary testimony, not a contemporaneous badge ledger.

## Postwar contributions: label separately

| Date | Documented milestone |
| --- | --- |
| 1948 | Returned to Los Alamos after teaching at Chicago; began the MANIAC development period. [2](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/profile/nicholas-metropolis/) |
| March 1952 | MANIAC began operation. Metropolis directed its construction. [2](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/profile/nicholas-metropolis/) |
| 1953 | Coauthored the paper associated with the Metropolis algorithm with **Arianna Rosenbluth, Marshall Rosenbluth, Augusta (Mici) Teller, and Edward Teller**. [5](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/20736632-marshall-rosenbluth-metropolis-algorithm) |

LANL credits him with a vital role in Monte Carlo simulation and leadership of the MANIAC team. This does **not** justify saying that he alone invented Monte Carlo or the algorithm bearing his name. Gubernatis's historical account discusses the coauthors and Marshall Rosenbluth's recollections; attribution merits a separate, careful treatment. [4](https://www.lanl.gov/media/publications/the-vault/0822-metropolis) [5](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/20736632-marshall-rosenbluth-metropolis-algorithm)

A minor unresolved discrepancy: the museum profile dates his senior fellowship to **1980**, while LANL's 2022 article says **1981**. This app update omits that milestone rather than silently selecting a date. [2](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/profile/nicholas-metropolis/) [4](https://www.lanl.gov/media/publications/the-vault/0822-metropolis)

## Archival leads and next research queue

1. **Metropolis badge provenance:** LANL describes a 211-box Metropolis collection, including papers, photographs, and later donated material containing his **last LANL badge**. Do not confuse this late-career artifact with the G-15 photograph. A catalog or archivist inquiry could establish whether an issue ledger or dated original survives; availability to the public has not been established. [4](https://www.lanl.gov/media/publications/the-vault/0822-metropolis)
2. **Stanley Frankel:** Metropolis names him in the ENIAC work. Next step: locate his badge image, transcribe its identifier, and establish chronology independently. No badge number added yet. [3](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/voices/oral-histories/nicholas-metropolis-interview/)
3. **Richard Feynman:** enrich the existing I-11 record with their calculator-repair and punched-card collaboration, carefully distinguishing recollection from contemporary documentation. [3](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/voices/oral-histories/nicholas-metropolis-interview/)
4. **John von Neumann and Stanislaw Ulam:** investigate the connections described by Metropolis, but keep later Monte Carlo work separate from wartime badge evidence. [3](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/voices/oral-histories/nicholas-metropolis-interview/) [4](https://www.lanl.gov/media/publications/the-vault/0822-metropolis)
5. **Arianna and Marshall Rosenbluth:** essential to a later computing-history exhibit; the 1953 paper alone is not evidence of Manhattan Project service or of a wartime badge. [5](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/20736632-marshall-rosenbluth-metropolis-algorithm)

### Follow-up: Frankel investigated

The next research pass added [Stanley P. Frankel, badge O-2](los-alamos-frankel.md), with a separate alternate photograph left untranscribed. Metropolis’s 1987 published account explicitly places the Moore School visit in March 1945 and the ENIAC results review in spring 1946. This refines the chronology beyond the 1993 interview summary without implying that the computations were finished during the war. [6](https://mcnp.lanl.gov/pdf_files/Article_1987_LAS_Metropolis_125--130.pdf)
