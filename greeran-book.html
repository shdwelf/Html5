I
Editorial premise

How to make a balanced book from an uneven record

The public record around Steven Thomas Greeran is uneven. Officially, the strongest and clearest documentation concerns a California conviction later referenced by Virgin Islands authorities, his registration history in the U.S. Virgin Islands, and the 2025 charge for alleged registration noncompliance.12 Separately, a self-published web resume supplies a much broader but less authoritative map of schooling, work, and volunteer activity across Southern California and Santa Barbara County.9

A third and fourth body of material now joins the file. A subject-supplied family-history research record — the Seize Quartiers genealogy file and an interactive military-service map — extends the book backward across seven generations and two countries, from Drumgooland in County Down to Revolutionary Pennsylvania.1011 And a growing shelf of self-published standalone HTML5 tools extends it forward, documenting a working cryptography, censorship-analysis, audio-DSP, naturalist, and genealogy toolset of the 2020s — including one artifact that presents the resume itself as an RC4-encrypted puzzle.122026 Both arrive labeled as self-published or research-file material, and both are kept on their own rung of the evidence ladder.

This book model treats those two evidence classes differently. Official records anchor the legal chronology. Resume material fills out the earlier life-and-work trail without pretending to know motives, feelings, or unwritten episodes. The result is not a traditional memoir, but it is a more balanced timeline than a dossier limited only to the 2025 case.9

The point of this edition is not to romanticize or to flatten the record. It is to keep the whole available timeline in view while labeling what is official, what is self-published, and what remains unverified.

Emergency! — the real connection

Emergency! premiered on NBC on January 15, 1972 and ran 124 episodes through 1977. It was the last show created by Jack Webb (Dragnet, Adam-12). The fictional "Station 51" was actually Fire Station 127 at 2049 East 223rd Street in Carson, California, chosen for its natural lighting by co-creator Robert Cinader. The real dispatcher heard on the show was Sam Lanier, an actual LACoFD dispatcher with over 18 years' service. The character of John Gage was based on Battalion Chief Jim Page, who helped create the LACoFD paramedic program.

When the show debuted, only 12 paramedic squads existed in the entire United States. By the time it ended first-run production, more than half of all Americans were within 4–7 minutes of advanced life-saving care. The show was one of the most successful public health education campaigns in American history.

"KMG365" — the call sign Station 51's crew uses to acknowledge dispatch — is a real FCC station license assigned to LACoFD Fire Station 98 in Bellflower. It appears on the station patch for Station 127.

Chantry Flat — Big Santa Anita Canyon

The family's mountain connection traces to Chantry Flat in the Big Santa Anita Canyon, Angeles National Forest. Originally known as "Poison Oak Flat," it was renamed after prospector Charley Chantry, who arrived in 1905. During the Great Hiking Era (1890s–1920s), over 220 cabins were built in the canyon. Materials were hauled in by mules and people's backs on narrow mountain trails — pack trains traveled seven days a week during the busy construction years. The Chantry Road to the trailhead wasn't built until 1935; before that, you hiked from Sierra Madre, adding four miles each way.

Great-grandfather brought the family up to Angeles Crest campground on top of the ridge, where they built huts — materials packed up by mule from Chantry Flats. They lived in cabins at Chantry Flats. Today about 80 cabins remain, privately owned on USFS land leases, with no electricity or utilities — everything still brought in by mule. The canyon has the last magneto-type crank phone system in the United States. Adams Pack Station, operating since 1936, is the last remaining pack station in Southern California — and it's where mail and supplies come from. Sturtevant Camp, founded in 1893, is one of the oldest resorts in the San Gabriels.

The cabins have survived the 1938 flood, the 1953 Monrovia Peak Fire, the 1969 flood, and the 2020 Bobcat Fire (which destroyed 18 of the 80 remaining cabins). They cannot be rebuilt if lost — a living museum to early 20th-century Southern California mountain life.

JPL, Caltech & the Aerojet connection

Morris Dam — torpedo testing in the San Gabriel Mountains

Just a few miles north of Azusa, the same Caltech/JPL scientists ran the Morris Reservoir Naval Weapons Test Site — a torpedo testing facility at Morris Dam on the San Gabriel River. Built in 1934 by Pasadena for water supply, the 245-foot concrete dam impounds a 1.5-mile-long, 160-foot-deep reservoir.

In 1943, Caltech established the test site for the U.S. Navy as a substation of the Naval Ordnance Test Station at China Lake. Caltech managed operations from 1943 to 1950. Testing focused on torpedoes, depth bombs, and submarine-launched weapons — including the Polaris missile. The Variable Angle Launcher (VAL) was the only such structure in the nation: a 300-foot welded steel frame firing torpedoes at up to 680 mph into the reservoir, adjustable from 5 to 38 degrees entry angle.

Caltech graduate student Conway Snyder — later a JPL scientist who spent 30 years on lunar and interplanetary exploration — accidentally launched the world's first underwater rocket from a rowboat at Morris Dam. Testing whether rockets would produce bubble trails underwater, the sixth rocket shot out of the water and through the air. This mishap helped lead to the development of the submarine-launched Polaris ICBM.

The torpedo tests "paid off for the fleet with the tremendous victories won by Navy aviators at the Battle of Leyte Gulf in October 1944" — 60 enemy ships sunk. Testing continued until June 1993, decommissioned under BRAC in 1997. The concrete ski-jump launch ramp is still visible from Highway 39. In October 2024, Morris Dam was designated a National Historic Engineering Landmark on its 90th anniversary.

Self-published works · a motif and what it became

The ensō line: a brushstroke that became a key

◆ Self-published works — author-attributed. The artifacts are citable; the reading of them below is editorial, not a sourced claim about the subject's beliefs.

Five artifacts supplied with the fifth resource batch share one figure. The ensō — the circle brushed in a single stroke in Zen calligraphy, traditionally left open, the gap part of the form rather than a flaw in it — appears first as a drawing and ends as a key.5455565758

The progression is visible build by build. The first generator draws a circle from a seed ID and exports it as a PNG; a "random terrain" control sets the same stroke against a generated landscape.54 The second build adds one field — custom seed (optional) — and with it the ensō stops being only an image: any phrase can now determine the circle.55 That is the hinge of the whole line.

The next three builds walk through the opened door. BIP-39 Haiku Wallet & Ensō Forge mines for a seed phrase that is simultaneously a checksum-valid BIP-39 mnemonic and a metrically exact 5-7-5 haiku, and draws the ensō from the same parameters — "mining ensures exact 5-7-5 syllables with a valid BIP39 SHA-256 checksum."56 Ensō Haiku Wallet Forge instruments every variable — haiku, mnemonic, BIP-44 path, xpub, P2PKH address, SHA-256 wallet fingerprint, ensō parameters — and collapses the whole set into a single printable Ensō Reference Number, base-62 encoded: "unique seed encoding all parameters." The circle becomes the record.57 The last build makes the line offline: rather than fetching the wordlist, the user pastes the official 2048 words once and they are "permanently committed to your browser's local sandbox storage so that all cryptographic operations can run fully offline," behind an AES-256-GCM vault and a batch miner with a grammar filter that "automatically skips awkward poetic line breaks."58

Two readings are available and the record does not settle between them. The first is mechanical: a workshop that needed a deterministic, offline, human-memorable seed found that poetry — five, seven, five syllables — makes a better mnemonic than twelve arbitrary words, and that a brush circle makes a better fingerprint than a hex string. The second is temperamental, and it has a thread back through this book: Chapter VI records volunteer entries at meditation centers — Dhammakaya, Sunnataram — and the same subject who put that practice on a resume later spent five builds on the signature figure of Zen calligraphy.954 This edition states the first as artifact and the second as interpretation; it does not claim the tools were made as practice, because nothing published says so. The motif's persistence across five independent builds is the only evidence offered here.

The same figure runs through the workshop's other output: the repository this book ships in carries an art studio whose own build name is Ensō Forge, and the Unified Utility Suite catalogues an Ensō generator alongside the open-source tools it curates.46 Like everything in Chapters IX and X, these are artifacts, not endorsements: the cryptographic properties are as-implemented and unaudited, and the tools say so themselves — "for educational/demonstration purposes. Do not use for storing real funds without independent verification."57

The stroke that is not closed is still the stroke. In this workshop the circle with a gap in it became the thing that closes a key.

XII
Gaps & private prompts

What remains outside the published edition

Several details requested for expansion — such as named dorm residents, apartment anecdotes, exact class schedules, specific social stories, and many travel memories — were not verified in the public sources reviewed for this project. Publishing them as fact would blur the line between biography and invention, and in some cases would identify third parties who are not public figures.

The family-history chapters show how promotion works when documents do arrive: the Seize Quartiers file left exactly two ancestral slots open — the Texas parents of Daisy Beatrice and the Morin parents of Biddeford, Maine — each with a named certificate (California marriage ~1921–22, Texas birth 1904, California death 1989, Maine vital records) that would close it.10 Locate the record, cite it, and the slot moves from evidenced to proven. That is the same workflow this book asks of every other memory.

One disagreement is deliberately left standing rather than resolved: a second, later seize quartiers — the Family Tree Visualization & Query Workbench — agrees with the research file on the Dechomet core but swaps several other slots (a Down/England William Hewitt for the proven San Francisco Fred Leslie Hewett; a Texas Carey line for Luther Gordon and Joel Thornburg Carey; James Greeran × Mary Hillen for the proven John Hillan × Catherine Dogherty) while uniquely naming the Morin couples of Biddeford — François Morin × Eugénie Bérubé and Alfred Houle × Eugénie Tremblay.1045 The open task: pull the Maine vital records and the Texas/California certificates, test each contested slot against primary documents, and let the certificates decide which file survives — both are preserved verbatim in the resource archive so the comparison can be rerun at any time.

For that reason, this app separates public book pages from a private embedded vault. Use the vault to capture reminders, chapter fragments, timelines, and personal prompts while the published book remains sourced. If supporting material is later provided, those details can be promoted from private notes into the visible book with clear labeling.

Recommended workflow

  1. Keep verified life facts in the visible book pages.
  2. Keep unsourced memories in the vault until documented or intentionally labeled personal recollection.
  3. Export the vault JSON periodically as a backup while you work.
