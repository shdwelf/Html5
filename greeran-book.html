I
Editorial premise

How to make a balanced book from an uneven record

The public record around Steven Thomas Greeran is uneven. Officially, the strongest and clearest documentation concerns a California conviction later referenced by Virgin Islands authorities, his registration history in the U.S. Virgin Islands, and the 2025 charge for alleged registration noncompliance.12 Separately, a self-published web resume supplies a much broader but less authoritative map of schooling, work, and volunteer activity across Southern California and Santa Barbara County.9

This book model treats those two evidence classes differently. Official records anchor the legal chronology. Resume material fills out the earlier life-and-work trail without pretending to know motives, feelings, or unwritten episodes. The result is not a traditional memoir, but it is a more balanced timeline than a dossier limited only to the 2025 case.9

The point of this edition is not to romanticize or to flatten the record. It is to keep the whole available timeline in view while labeling what is official, what is self-published, and what remains unverified.

Personal recollection · Glendora childhood

Growing up at 2860, the Landers soccer team, and Seller's violin

⚠ Personal recollection — not independently verified

The house was at 2860 in Glendora. The room was small and in the back — a kid's room, the kind of space where the world gets built from whatever is within arm's reach. This is where the earliest personal memories begin: not from any published source, but from lived experience in a San Gabriel Valley suburb that shaped a generation of school mornings, blacktop lunches, and after-school routines.

Soccer came first among organized activities. The team was the Landers, and the position was halfback. Teammate Chris Griffin was on the squad — a name that survives in memory even when so many others have faded. Being halfback meant running the midfield, connecting defense to attack, doing the work that rarely made the highlight reel but kept the game moving. Then came the eye exam and the discovery: near-sighted. The world had always been slightly soft at the edges, and suddenly there was a reason.

At school — likely linked to the Glendora Unified School District — students were required to learn a musical instrument. The instrument chosen was violin, and the place was Seller's. There was also the cafeteria job: the milk man. Fifty cents for a box of milk. It was a small hustle, a way to earn and to belong, and then the bell would ring and it was out to the blacktop to eat. The blacktop lunch was a universal Southern California school experience — and the seagulls would come inland from the beach, wheeling over the campus looking for scraps, proof that the ocean was never as far away as it seemed.

Family connections reached into the school district itself. Aunt Lila — wife of Uncle Chuck — worked as Dr. Bushman's secretary at the district office of the Glendora Unified School District. That kind of proximity meant the district was not an abstraction; it was a place where familiar names showed up, where the adult world and the student world overlapped in small, ordinary ways.

And then there was the writing. An awards ceremony held in the board room at the state level: an honorable mention for a story. The story was about Battalion Chief Robert Hewitt of the Los Angeles County Fire Department — stationed not in Temple City but in El Monte. That story became a thread that connected childhood, family history, and something much larger.

David Seubert, wax cylinders, Z39.50 & the California Digital Library

The user worked for David Seubert, curator of special collections / Performing Arts Curator at UCSB Library. Seubert went to Oberlin. Under his leadership, the UCSB Cylinder Audio Archive became one of the world's largest collections of wax cylinder recordings — over 16,000 recordings, close to 11,000 online. The first library with a big collection of early sound recordings online that the public could download for free. Started in 2002 as a pilot project (60 cylinders); the full archive launched autumn 2005 with 5,000 newly digitized recordings. The library uses the Archeophone (designed 1998 by Frenchman Henri Chamoux) to convert the grooves of wax cylinders into digital data.

The user connected the Apple Darwin wax cylinder recordings to Z39.50 PHP to send queries to and from the Pegasus system that syncs with the Library of Congress MELVYL system. Z39.50: the standard protocol for bibliographic information retrieval. MELVYL: the UC system's union catalog. The California Digital Library (CDL) emerged from efforts to enhance MELVYL — founded in 1997 by the UC Board of Regents and President Richard Atkinson, who called it a "library without walls." Clifford Lynch, head of the Division of Library Automation for the UC system, presented the draft plan. CDL's vision: building, sharing, and preserving digital collections; creating tools and services; influencing scholarly communication; fostering strategic partnerships.

Camouflaged Burbank, hidden Azusa, secret Oildale

Boots is Norma Rowley — confirmed. The parallel runs deeper: just as Lockheed's Burbank plant was disguised during WWII as a fake suburban neighborhood — burlap houses, wire-and-feather trees, rubber automobiles, painted streets, with Hollywood studios (MGM, Disney, 20th Century Fox) helping build the illusion — the facility in Azusa was making space shuttle components hidden in plain sight. Colonel John F. Ohmer led the Burbank camouflage; when he flew a general over at 5,000 feet, the general said he could only see "suburb after California suburb." Warner Brothers even disguised a nearby studio to look like the aircraft plant as a decoy.

The same pattern: the U-2 spy plane was built at a secret Lockheed facility on Norris Road in Oildale (Bakersfield), disguised as a tire factory. Called "Unit 80," the first 50 U-2s were assembled there in 1956–1957, then partially disassembled and flown at night to Groom Lake (Area 51) for flight testing. A Government Printing Office / NASA book — Unlimited Horizons by Peter Merlin — documents the U-2 history.

Beale Air Force Base — home of the U-2 Dragon Lady since 1976, named after Edward Fitzgerald Beale, a 19th-century pioneer. The "high flyer named after their football team." All 27 U-2s operated from Beale. The plane can fly above 70,000 feet — still in service 70+ years after its first flight. The names have been changed.

Personal recollection · The Hewitt family

Battalion Chief Robert Hewitt, Chuck Hewitt, Agent Orange, and a kidney

⚠ Personal recollection — not independently verified

The story that won the honorable mention was nominally about Battalion Chief Robert Hewitt of the Los Angeles County Fire Department, stationed at El Monte. But the real story — the family story — was deeper and more complicated than a grade-school writing award could contain. It was about Chuck Hewitt and Agent Orange.

Before Robert was a captain in Temple City, he was a hotshot for the fire crew — probably where they all met. The Dalton Hotshots, organized in September 1953 in Glendora, were based at 1090 Glendora Mountain Road. The original crew: foreman Charles (Chuck) Hartley and 22 Native American firefighters — 11 Jemez and 11 Zia men from Northern New Mexico. Their mascot: the Bighorn Ram, inspired by the wild sheep of the San Gabriel Mountains. Other Angeles Forest hotshot crews: Little Tujunga (est. 1970), Bear Divide, Valyermo, Texas Canyon. The El Cariso Hotshots lost 12 crewmen in the Loop Fire, November 1, 1966, in Pacoima Canyon.

The connection to fire service ran through the family. Grandpa — the one whose house you stayed at, listening to the radio and watching Emergency! on television — had served in the fire department. The show's dispatch sequences were not fiction to him; they echoed LACOFD dispatch, the actual radio traffic of the Los Angeles County Fire Department. When the uniform service brought replacement gear, Grandpa gave his dress uniform, badge, and clothes to keep. Those were not costumes — they were artifacts of a career.

Emergency! — the real connection

Emergency! premiered on NBC on January 15, 1972 and ran 124 episodes through 1977. It was the last show created by Jack Webb (Dragnet, Adam-12). The fictional "Station 51" was actually Fire Station 127 at 2049 East 223rd Street in Carson, California, chosen for its natural lighting by co-creator Robert Cinader. The real dispatcher heard on the show was Sam Lanier, an actual LACoFD dispatcher with over 18 years' service. The character of John Gage was based on Battalion Chief Jim Page, who helped create the LACoFD paramedic program.

When the show debuted, only 12 paramedic squads existed in the entire United States. By the time it ended first-run production, more than half of all Americans were within 4–7 minutes of advanced life-saving care. The show was one of the most successful public health education campaigns in American history.

"KMG365" — the call sign Station 51's crew uses to acknowledge dispatch — is a real FCC station license assigned to LACoFD Fire Station 98 in Bellflower. It appears on the station patch for Station 127.

Maurice had a workbench at his house in Duarte — not a shop, but the place where the user used to hang out and learn soldering. Maurice made terminals that went in fire engines for "texting dispatch" — custom-built Mobile Data Terminals (MDTs) connecting responding crews to Computer-Aided Dispatch systems. In the early days, many MDTs were custom devices, even specialized point-to-point radios. A good example of the dispatch archives: WildCAD for the Angeles National Forest — a computer-aided dispatch system used by wildland fire agencies nationwide. It's federal (USFS), so the archives might go back to early digital dispatch records.

Maurice Frederick Hewitt — ham radio callsign KG6ZD — was another son of Robert. The ham radio world was its own kind of fire service: a network of voices on the air, people listening and responding, a community built around the ability to reach out across distance with nothing but a signal and a frequency.

The hardest part of the family story involved Chuck Hewitt, Robert's oldest son. Chuck's health had been compromised — the connection to Agent Orange exposure hangs over many families of that era. When his kidneys began to fail, the choice was dialysis or transplant. Robert Hewitt gave his oldest son a kidney. It was not a metaphor. It was a father literally giving a part of himself so his son could live without the machine.

This is the story underneath the story: a fire-service family, a father and his sons, the long chemical shadow of a war, a kidney given in place of dialysis, and a callsign on the ham radio band — KG6ZD — that kept transmitting.

The award-winning story was about Battalion Chief Robert Hewitt. The real story was about what a family carries — in uniform, in blood, on the air.

Chantry Flat — Big Santa Anita Canyon

The family's mountain connection traces to Chantry Flat in the Big Santa Anita Canyon, Angeles National Forest. Originally known as "Poison Oak Flat," it was renamed after prospector Charley Chantry, who arrived in 1905. During the Great Hiking Era (1890s–1920s), over 220 cabins were built in the canyon. Materials were hauled in by mules and people's backs on narrow mountain trails — pack trains traveled seven days a week during the busy construction years. The Chantry Road to the trailhead wasn't built until 1935; before that, you hiked from Sierra Madre, adding four miles each way.

Great-grandfather brought the family up to Angeles Crest campground on top of the ridge, where they built huts — materials packed up by mule from Chantry Flats. They lived in cabins at Chantry Flats. Today about 80 cabins remain, privately owned on USFS land leases, with no electricity or utilities — everything still brought in by mule. The canyon has the last magneto-type crank phone system in the United States. Adams Pack Station, operating since 1936, is the last remaining pack station in Southern California — and it's where mail and supplies come from. Sturtevant Camp, founded in 1893, is one of the oldest resorts in the San Gabriels.

The cabins have survived the 1938 flood, the 1953 Monrovia Peak Fire, the 1969 flood, and the 2020 Bobcat Fire (which destroyed 18 of the 80 remaining cabins). They cannot be rebuilt if lost — a living museum to early 20th-century Southern California mountain life.

Nike missile sites — the San Gabriel ridge defense perimeter

During the Cold War, 16 Nike missile sites surrounded Los Angeles in the "Ring of Supersonic Steel" — designed to intercept Soviet bombers before they could reach the city. The San Gabriel Mountains ridge was lined with missile sites, designed to lay cover for Los Angeles to retreat — a defensive perimeter built into the mountain skyline.

Key sites in the Angeles National Forest: Mount Gleason (LA-04) — first Nike site in the Angeles, active 1955–1974, later became LACoFD Camp 16 (destroyed in the 2009 Station Fire). Los Pinetos/Newhall (LA-94) — 4,000 feet above the San Fernando Valley, 1955–1968. Barley Flats/Mount Disappointment (LA-09) and Magic Mountain/Lang (LA-98). Closer to home: South El Monte (LA-14) near Whittier Narrows, active 1956–1961. All decommissioned by 1974 under the SALT agreement.

Aerojet — Grandfather Bernard in Azusa

Grandfather Bernard worked at Aerojet in Azusa, California — the rocket engine manufacturing facility at 1100 W Hollyvale St, Azusa, CA 91702. Aerojet was founded in 1942 and moved to Azusa in January 1943, producing JATO (Jet-Assisted TakeOff) units and rocket engines for the military. Bernard had security clearance — classified defense work during the Cold War.

Aerojet sold the Azusa facility in 2001 to Northrop Grumman Corporation, which now operates it as part of its Space Systems division. The facility transitioned from rocket production into R&D, optics, and infrared detectors for satellite-based projects.

The Azusa site was declared an EPA Superfund Site in 1985 (San Gabriel Superfund Site II) after TCE contamination was discovered in groundwater in 1980. In 1997, NDMA and ammonium perchlorate contamination were also found. Cleanup continues under the Baldwin Park Operable Unit.

Aerojet's environmental footprint extends across multiple states: the Rancho Cordova facility near Sacramento (8,500 acres, Superfund since 1983, 27-square-mile groundwater contamination plume); Chino Hills (800-acre ordnance facility, munitions and depleted uranium cleanup); and Homestead, Florida — near the Everglades, where a 1963 rocket test went wrong: the nozzle ejected, spreading hydrochloric acid across wetlands, crop fields, and homes. The massive AJ-260-2 rocket motor still sits in its silo today.

JPL, Caltech & the Aerojet connection

Aerojet and JPL were born from the same group. In 1936, Caltech graduate student Frank Malina and self-taught rocket enthusiasts Jack Parsons and Edward Forman — the "Suicide Squad" — tested their first rocket motor in the Arroyo Seco on Halloween 1936. Their advisor, Theodore von Kármán, secured Army funding for JATO (Jet-Assisted Take-Off) rockets.

In 1943, Parsons, Malina, Forman, and von Kármán established the Aerojet Engineering Corporation to manufacture JATO motors. JPL was officially named in November 1943. Aerojet moved to Azusa; JPL stayed in Pasadena. Bernard worked in the same building where Caltech/JPL scientists worked.

Morris Dam — torpedo testing in the San Gabriel Mountains

Just a few miles north of Azusa, the same Caltech/JPL scientists ran the Morris Reservoir Naval Weapons Test Site — a torpedo testing facility at Morris Dam on the San Gabriel River. Built in 1934 by Pasadena for water supply, the 245-foot concrete dam impounds a 1.5-mile-long, 160-foot-deep reservoir.

In 1943, Caltech established the test site for the U.S. Navy as a substation of the Naval Ordnance Test Station at China Lake. Caltech managed operations from 1943 to 1950. Testing focused on torpedoes, depth bombs, and submarine-launched weapons — including the Polaris missile. The Variable Angle Launcher (VAL) was the only such structure in the nation: a 300-foot welded steel frame firing torpedoes at up to 680 mph into the reservoir, adjustable from 5 to 38 degrees entry angle.

Caltech graduate student Conway Snyder — later a JPL scientist who spent 30 years on lunar and interplanetary exploration — accidentally launched the world's first underwater rocket from a rowboat at Morris Dam. Testing whether rockets would produce bubble trails underwater, the sixth rocket shot out of the water and through the air. This mishap helped lead to the development of the submarine-launched Polaris ICBM.

The torpedo tests "paid off for the fleet with the tremendous victories won by Navy aviators at the Battle of Leyte Gulf in October 1944" — 60 enemy ships sunk. Testing continued until June 1993, decommissioned under BRAC in 1997. The concrete ski-jump launch ramp is still visible from Highway 39. In October 2024, Morris Dam was designated a National Historic Engineering Landmark on its 90th anniversary.
